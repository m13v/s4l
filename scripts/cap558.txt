here is a story.

i was 44. seventeen years as the archivist at a county historical society. basement reading room, humming dehumidifier, gray acid-free boxes on steel shelves. i did not just file paper. i knew which family donated letters and quietly kept the ugly ones, which ledger had pages razored out, which photo was labeled wrong on purpose. i believed the work lived in that knowing, not in the cataloging.

i said it on the record at a board meeting last fall. word for word: "a machine can read a document. it can't tell you what it means." the minutes have it. i felt safe for about a month.

then the board ran an ai pass over the backlog one weekend. every unprocessed box, transcribed, dated, indexed, names cross-referenced across a century. work i had scheduled out past my retirement. it was not perfect. it dated a letter by its printed letterhead and missed that the paper had been reused decades later, the exact kind of thing i catch. but the index was sitting in my inbox monday morning.

box 212 was in that backlog. a gray box of mill correspondence i had been meaning to process for eleven years. the ai finished it before lunch.

i stayed after close that night, box 212 open on the reading table, tea gone cold, reading its summary against the originals, hunting for proof the room still needed me.

i found it in box 212, but not in anything the ai had read. the letters stopped for three years, right around a strike. no letter says why. the index was flawless and blind to the silence. only someone who knew the shelf would notice the gap.

so i changed what i do. the ai transcribes and indexes now, faster than i ever could. i work the gaps: what's missing, who took it out, what the silence is hiding. box 212 has a new finding aid, and half of it is about what isn't there.

the lesson is the catalog was never the job. reading is free now. the job was knowing what the box is missing.

stop cataloging what's in the box. start asking what's missing from it.

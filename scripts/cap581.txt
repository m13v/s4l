here is a story.

i was 46. twenty two years calling estate auctions, barns and church halls and living rooms full of somebody's whole life on folding tables. people think the job is talking fast. it isn't. it's reading the room. knowing which bidder is about to fold, and holding the pause one breath longer so they don't.

a few years back i spoke at a county fair benefit, mic in hand, and said it on the record: a machine can't read a room. it can count bids. it'll never see a man's jaw tighten before he drops out. people clapped. the local paper printed it.

then last spring the auction house moved most sales online. they showed me the new system in the back office. it set the increments and timed the going once, going twice. the owner smiled and said it learned its timing from twenty two years of my recorded sales. my tapes. my pauses.

i watched it run a test lot. it held the pause exactly where i would have. same breath. same little lift before the hammer. i sat in my truck in the gravel lot after with the heater ticking and didn't start the engine for a long time. it was my voice without me in it.

a month later it ran a farm estate. two brothers bid against each other on their late dad's tractor. the system did what it learned. it held the pause, nudged the increment, and kept them climbing. i would have stopped the sale, walked over, and let the younger one have it. one brother won. they haven't spoken since.

so my work changed. the system runs the online increments now and it's good at it. i walk the floor before the sale. i talk to the families. i decide which lots get pulled, which get sold quietly, which ones should never hit the block at all.

the lesson: the machine learned when to pause. it never learned when to stop.

stop calling the bids. start reading the family.

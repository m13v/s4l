here is a story.

i was 52. twenty four years authenticating paintings for an auction house. a canvas comes in with a family story and a dusty frame, and you stand close enough to smell it. the brushwork, the way the paint sits on the weave, how a hand hesitates on a horizon. people think it's a lab job. it isn't. it's an eye, and a signature at the bottom of a certificate that says this is real.

when the house started testing a model trained on high resolution scans of brushstrokes, they asked me to write a short piece for the catalog. i put it in print: a model can't see a hand. it sees texture. a forger can fake texture. only an eye that has looked at ten thousand canvases knows when the hand is wrong. they ran it on page four.

that winter they pointed the model at our own archive. every canvas we had certified in twenty years. it flagged eleven. one of them was a small river landscape i signed for in my second year. i remembered it. i had been proud of it. the lab took a sample from the sky. titanium white, the kind nobody could buy until decades after the painter died.

i went down to the archive basement and pulled the file myself. the dehumidifier hummed. there was my signature, younger, rounder, very sure. i stood there holding it for a long time. the model hadn't beaten me that week. it had beaten me eleven years ago, and i'd been the last one to find out.

so my work changed. every canvas goes under the scanner first now. it reads strokes faster and colder than i ever did. i do the part it can't: tracing the provenance, calling the family, sitting with a widow who believes her husband's painting is real, deciding what goes to the lab and what gets said out loud.

i called the buyer of the river landscape myself. he was kinder than i deserved.

the lesson: my eye wasn't the product. my signature was. and a signature only means something if you let someone check it.

stop defending the eye. start auditing the signature.

here is a story.

i was 54. twenty-seven years as a picture editor at a newspaper. every night the photographers dumped four, five hundred frames into the system, and my job was to pick the one that ran on the front. not the sharpest one. the true one. the fire where the firefighter is looking back at the house, not at the flames. i could scroll a take at speed and stop dead on the frame that told the whole story. i thought that eye was the last thing a machine would ever get.

last spring the paper set up a blind test. same take, a model picks its top three, i pick mine, the night desk votes without knowing whose is whose. i agreed out loud, in front of the whole newsroom. i said let it try, i will be home by eleven.

it was not close. on a flood take it pulled the woman carrying a dog through water up to her waist, cropped tighter than i would have, and the desk picked it over mine five votes to two. on a city council take it found a councilman mid-yawn i had scrolled right past. it did the whole night in under a minute. i had needed three hours.

i drove home after midnight with the radio off and sat in the car in the driveway. i kept replaying the vote. then i remembered the frame it had ranked number one on the car crash take. it was the best picture of the night, technically. it also showed a kid's face, still readable, the night before his parents would be told. the model had no idea that was the problem. the desk had not noticed either.

i did not quit. i changed what i do. the model sorts the take now, all five hundred frames, and hands me a shortlist. i spend my night on the calls it cannot make: whose face we do not show, which true picture is too cruel to run, what a family sees at breakfast.

the lesson is picking the best frame was never the job. finding the best frame is sorting, and sorting is cheap now. the job was deciding what we owe the people inside the picture.

stop picking the sharpest frame. start deciding which ones should never run.

here is a story.

i was 41. nineteen years teaching chess to kids, saturday mornings in a church basement, folding tables, plastic pieces, a clock that clicked too loud. i did not just teach openings. i knew which kid played fast because they were scared, which one resigned early because losing at home was worse than losing here, which one needed a hard game and which one needed a win. i believed the coaching lived in that knowing, not in the moves.

i said it out loud at a parents night last winter. word for word: "an engine can find the best move. it can't teach a nine year old why it's the best move." a room full of parents nodded. i felt safe for about a month.

then the kids started bringing tablets. an ai tutor that plays them, explains every mistake in plain words at their level, as many times as they want, at 11pm on a school night. it was patient in a way i am not by my fourth hour. it was not perfect. it praised a move that only worked because the other kid missed something, the exact thing i used to catch.

the question came from maya, eleven, packing up her pieces. "if the app explains it better, what are you for?"

i did not have an answer. i drove home and sat in my kitchen with cold tea and her game still set up on the board, hunting for proof the room still needed me.

i found it in her game, not in the engine's notes. she played the whole middlegame well and fell apart the second she was ahead. no engine flags that. it grades the move. it doesn't notice the kid is afraid of winning.

so i changed what i do. the tutor handles the explaining now, all week, as patient as they need. saturdays are for what it can't see: the nerves, the tilt after a blunder, the kid who stops trying the moment the game gets close. i coach the player, not the position.

the lesson is the explanation was never the job. explaining is free now. the job was noticing the person holding the pieces.

stop explaining every move. start watching who's making them.

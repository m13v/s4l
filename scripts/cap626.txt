here is a story.

i was 58. twenty-six years inspecting sewer pipe. a little camera on wheels crawls down a main, and i sat in the back of a van watching the footage, coding every defect by hand. crack, root intrusion, offset joint, sag, collapse. each one got a code and a footage marker so the city knew which streets to dig up first.

i had my retirement speech half written. the big line was already in there, the one i'd been saying at conferences for years: "no machine will ever read a pipe. it takes a person who's seen ten thousand of them."

then the city bought software that codes the video on its own. they gave it our backlog one weekend. forty miles of footage that had been sitting on a drive for two years waiting for me to get to it.

by monday it was done. i spot checked it the way i'd check a new hire. the codes were right. and on a street i had inspected twice, it flagged a hairline fracture right at a joint, the kind that turns into a sinkhole. i went back to my own report. i had driven the camera past it both times. i had called it a stain.

i sat in the van with the laptop open and the speech in my bag, and that one line felt like something a stranger wrote.

so i changed how i work. the software codes every foot now. i review what it isn't sure about, the odd materials, the old brick lines from 1900 that nobody has a code for. and the rest of my time goes to the part nobody ever had time for: deciding which pipe to fix first, sitting with the engineers, walking the street with the crew before they dig.

i rewrote the speech. i kept one sentence about the pipes. "i spent twenty-six years being the bottleneck and called it expertise."

the lesson: the backlog was never proof you were needed. it was proof you were the slowest part.

stop guarding the queue. start emptying it.

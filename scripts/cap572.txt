here is a story.

i was 44. sixteen years building crosswords, the last six as the editor who picks which puzzles run. i read every submission myself, grid on paper, pencil behind my ear. people think a crossword is a word list. it isn't. it's a conversation with a stranger where every clue is a small joke you hope they catch at 7am.

in the spring of 2024 i wrote a column about the models. i said a machine can fill a grid, sure, it can't be clever. it doesn't know what a solver will groan at. i said i'd know one of its puzzles in three clues. a few hundred people shared it. two constructors emailed to thank me.

that fall i ran our annual contest, judged blind, names stripped. one puzzle stopped me. a theme i'd never seen, a clue that made me laugh out loud alone in my kitchen. i picked it as the winner. i wrote the constructor a note saying this is why humans still matter.

the constructor wrote back one line. the grid, the theme and most of the clues came from a model. they'd only fixed four clues and one crossing. they'd entered it because of my column.

i read it standing up. the dishwasher was running. my pencil was still behind my ear and i took it out and just held it.

i didn't pull the win. i called them instead, and asked which four clues they fixed. they were the four i'd circled as the weakest. the machine wrote the jokes. the human caught the ones that wouldn't land.

so i changed the job. i don't judge who made a puzzle anymore. i judge how many bad clues made it to my desk. model drafts are fine. the constructors who win now are the ones who kill their own clues before i ever see them.

the lesson: i said i'd spot the machine in three clues. i spotted the human in four.

stop guarding the grid. start grading the edit.

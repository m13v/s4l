here is a story.

i was 49. twenty-seven years grading tuna at the fish market, out on the cold floor at 4am with a flashlight and a coring tool my mentor handed me the day he retired. you read the tail cut: color, fat lines, sheen, the way the flesh gives under your thumb. i could price a fish in under a minute and the buyers trusted my tag over the paperwork.

when the market started piloting an app that scored tail-cut photos with a model, i said it to the whole auction room, on the record: "a camera can't read a tail cut. fat is something you feel. i'll eat that phone if it beats me on one fish."

three weeks later it flagged a fish i'd tagged top grade. it called the flesh burnt, heat damage from a long fight on the line, the kind that turns the meat soft and sour once it's cut. i laughed and let the buyer take it at my price.

the restaurant sent it back the next morning. cut open, the inside was gray and mushy, exactly where the app said. the buyer didn't yell. he just showed me the photo on his phone, then the one the app had taken, side by side.

i stood in the cold room after the auction holding my mentor's coring tool, ice melting off my boots, and realized i had been using his tool to defend my pride, not to read fish.

so i changed how i work. every tail gets photographed and scored before i walk the floor. i only core the fish the model isn't sure about, and i spend the rest of the morning on what a photo can't see: how the boat bled it, how it was iced, which captain cuts corners when the price is high. the new graders learn the tool from me now, but they learn when to use it from the scores.

the lesson: i thought the tool was the skill. the skill was knowing which fish to open.

stop proving your eye. start pointing it.

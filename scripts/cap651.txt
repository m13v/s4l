here is a story.

i was 47. fourteen years rating city streets for the public works department. i drove every block with a clipboard and a measuring wheel, got out at the bad ones, and scored the pavement one to ten. cracks, ruts, potholes, how deep, how wide. my scores decided which streets got repaved and which waited another year.

then the city put cameras on the garbage trucks and bought a model to score the footage. at the budget hearing, on the record, i said: "a camera at thirty miles an hour can't see depth. it will call a puddle a pothole and miss the one that eats a tire."

they ran us side by side for a year. i kept a binder. every street where i'd measured and the model had guessed, i wrote both scores down.

on any single street, on the day i drove it, i was more accurate. the binder proved it. i brought it to the review meeting and i was right.

then the engineer pulled up a different chart. not who scored better. when each pothole opened. i could cover the whole city once every three years. the trucks drove every street every week. a pothole that opened in march on a street i'd rated in january sat there until my next visit, two years and ten months later. the model logged it the first week, roughly right, and a crew patched it.

i sat in the truck in the yard with the binder on my lap. fourteen years of being precise about streets nobody had looked at since. the claims for blown tires had dropped by more than half. i was more accurate. the city was worse off with me.

now the trucks score everything. when the model flags a jump between two weeks, i drive out and measure that one block. i don't survey the city anymore. i check the changes. it's a smaller job and a better one.

a perfect reading every three years loses to a rough one every week. accuracy only counts when you're actually there to measure.

stop polishing the score. go count how often you show up.

here is a story.

i was 47. nineteen years transcribing jazz solos by ear for a small publisher of play-along books. same ritual every morning: coffee at six, headphones, a foot pedal that slowed the record to half speed, pencil on manuscript paper. one chorus a day if it went well.

at a summer jazz camp, a student asked the faculty panel about transcription apps. i took the mic and said, "an app can hear pitches. it can't hear what the player meant." the room clapped. the camp put the panel video online.

that fall the same student, now in my class, ran the solo i was working on through one of those apps. i had three mornings into it. the app gave her a full draft in under a minute. she brought it in to show me it was wrong.

it wasn't. it had caught a ghosted note i'd skipped and a swung figure i had flattened into straight eighths. i checked both at half speed on my pedal. it was right on both.

the next morning i sat down at six with the coffee and the headphones on and my foot over the pedal, and i just didn't press it. i sat there until the coffee went cold.

so i changed how i work. the app makes the first draft now. i spend my mornings on what it can't do: fingerings, breath marks, a note in the margin on why a phrase lands, and making students sing a line before they're allowed to read it.

the lesson: my ear was never the product. the explanation was. i'd spent nineteen years guarding the part a machine could do in a minute.

i still sit down at six with the coffee. headphones on. but now i'm reading, not rewinding.

stop rewinding. start explaining.
